# Characters

The **Characters** glyph in the tool rail, or the Cast view for the whole
window. The rail panel is for a quick look while you write; the Cast view is
where the three views below have room to be read.

![Characters panel](/planning/characters-panel.png)

Each person has a name, whatever description and notes you want to keep, a
role, and the chapters they appear in. The appearances are read out of the
manuscript, so they follow your writing rather than your bookkeeping — write
someone into a new scene and they turn up there without you recording it.

That is what makes the three views worth having. They are not three ways of
looking at a list you typed; they are three ways of looking at the book.

## Grid view

One row per character, one column per chapter, across the whole book.

A cell is tinted where that character is mentioned in that chapter, in their
own colour, and left empty where they are not. Hovering a cell names the
chapter and the number of mentions.

![Characters grid](/planning/characters-grid.png)

What it is for is **presence and absence at a glance**. A row that runs solid
for eight chapters and then stops has a character who left the book, and the
question is whether you meant that. A row with a single cell in chapter three
and nothing after is someone introduced with weight and then forgotten. Two
rows whose gaps line up exactly are two characters who never share a scene.

None of that is visible while reading, because reading is one chapter at a
time and this is the shape of the whole thing at once.

## Heatmap view

The same rows and columns, but the tint carries **how much**, not just
whether. A chapter with thirty mentions of someone is darker than a chapter
with two.

![Characters heatmap](/planning/characters-heatmap.png)

It sorts by mentions by default and adds a **role** column — protagonist,
antagonist, supporting, minor, other — which you set yourself.

Role is what makes the heatmap say something. Once your cast is labelled, the
question stops being "who is in chapter twelve" and becomes **"does the
weight match the intent"**. A protagonist whose band is pale through the
middle third is the middle of a book sagging, visible as a picture instead of
as a feeling. A minor character darker than your antagonist has taken over a
book you thought was about somebody else.

Mentions are a crude measure and worth treating as one. A character present in
a scene but not named is not counted, and a name repeated in dialogue counts
every time. Read it as a shape, not a score.

## Intertwined view

The other two show where people are. This one shows **what happens between
them**.

Each character is a line running left to right through the chapters, and the
lines are marked where the story does something: an introduction, a first
interaction, a discovery, a conflict, a turning point, a resolution. Marks are
coloured by what kind of moment they are, so a run of turning points reads
differently from a run of quiet state changes.

![Characters interweave](/planning/characters-interweave.png)

It answers the questions the grid cannot. When do these two actually meet?
How long after being introduced does she do anything? Is the confrontation you
think is at the middle of the book really at the middle of the book?

Hovering a mark says what it is and which chapter it is in, and you can jump
straight to that chapter from it.

Like everything derived from the manuscript, these are read out of your prose
rather than recorded by you, so they are a reading and not a record. It will
miss a confrontation written obliquely and it will find a turning point in an
argument about breakfast. It is a prompt to go and look, not a verdict.

## What Draftline works out on its own

It reads the manuscript for names and proposes people it thinks are in the
book, which saves typing a cast list by hand. Anything uncertain is badged
**REVIEW**.

It is a guess, and it is presented as one. You confirm or dismiss each
suggestion, and nothing joins your cast because software thought so. Expect it
to be confident about a place name that looks like a surname, and to miss
someone mentioned twice in ninety thousand words. Invented names are the
hardest case, which is unfortunate, because novels are full of them.

Corrections stick. Filter the list to **All candidates** to work through what
is still waiting on you.

## What it is not

It does not write character sheets, and it has no opinions about your
characters. It records who is in the book, where, and how often — and then
draws it three ways so you can see what you have actually written rather than
what you meant to.
